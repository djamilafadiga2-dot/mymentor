CV & Cover Letter

How to write a one-page UK finance CV and a cover letter that gets you through the first screen, even when your experience is still limited.

1. What recruiters look for

At spring level, your CV is often screened in under a minute. In that time it needs to prove three things:

  1. You are academically strong: good grades, awards, a demanding subject choice.
  2. You do things outside class: work experience, clubs, responsibility, initiative.
  3. You are genuinely interested in finance: an investment club, a mock portfolio, a competition, something you wrote.

Nobody expects a school student to have worked in a bank. What makes the difference is describing what you actually did in the experiences you have, with detail and numbers.

2. The UK CV format

A UK finance CV is one page, in English, in a simple black-and-white layout. It contains no photo and no personal details beyond how to contact you.

Include

  • Full name
  • Phone with country code (+225 …)
  • Professional email: firstname.lastname@gmail.com
  • LinkedIn URL
  • Font size 10–11: Garamond, Times New Roman or Calibri
  • Most recent first in every section

Leave out

  • Photo
  • Age, date of birth, nationality, marital status
  • Full postal address
  • A "Profile" or "Objective" paragraph
  • Colours, icons, two-column templates, skill bars (★★★☆)
  • "References available on request"

Section order

  1. Education
  2. Work Experience
  3. Leadership & Extracurricular Activities
  4. Projects & Achievements
  5. Skills, Languages & Interests

Save and send your CV as a PDF named Firstname_Lastname_CV.pdf. A Word file can shift its layout on the recruiter's screen.

3. Section by section

Every line on the page should show a skill firms want: analysis, teamwork, leadership, communication or initiative. Small experiences count when you describe what you did.

Education

  • School name, city, dates (with "expected" for the end date), and your qualification and subjects: e.g. Baccalauréat — Mathematics, Economics & Social Sciences.
  • Your average and your class rank if it is strong: "Average: 16.4/20 (ranked 3rd of 45)".
  • Awards, honours, scholarships and competitions.
  • Your English level with proof if you have it (IELTS, Cambridge).

Work Experience

  • Include every work placement you have done at school, even one week long. Give the role in English: "Work Experience Student, Finance Department".
  • Give the company, city and dates, and say "(1 week)" if that is the length. Being honest about length is expected.
  • Write 2–3 bullets about what you did, not what you watched. Did you sort invoices, build a table in Excel, sit in on a client meeting, write a report at the end?
  • A placement outside finance still counts. A week at a pharmacy, law firm or telecoms company shows customer contact, organisation and professional behaviour.

Leadership & Extracurricular Activities

  • Clubs (debating, investment or economics, Model UN, journalism), sports teams, music, volunteering, community or faith groups.
  • Give your role and what changed because of you: "Treasurer: managed a 150,000 FCFA budget for 3 events."
  • Two activities with detail beat six listed with none.

Projects & Achievements

This is where a school student can stand out. Projects that work well for finance:

  • A mock stock portfolio tracked over 3 months, with a short write-up of each decision.
  • A small business: selling at school, running an online shop, organising a paid event.
  • Something you wrote: a LinkedIn article or blog post on a company, a stock market, or inflation in your country.
  • Online courses with a certificate.
  • Competitions: maths olympiads, business plan or pitch competitions.

Skills, Languages & Interests

  • Languages with a level: French (native), English (fluent, IELTS 7.0).
  • Technical: Excel, PowerPoint, any coding. Only list what you could be tested on in an interview.
  • Interests: be specific. "Football" says nothing; "Captain of the school football team, 2 seasons" gives the interviewer something to ask about.

4. How to write a bullet point

Every bullet follows one formula:

Action verb + what you did + result or number

Start with a past-tense verb, keep it to one or two lines, and add a number wherever you honestly can.

WeakStrong
Work experience at a bank.Shadowed 3 client advisers at a retail bank branch and summarised how loan applications are assessed in a 2-page report.
I helped in the accounting department.Sorted and filed 200+ supplier invoices by due date, helping the team prepare month-end payments.
Member of the debating club.Debated in 6 inter-school competitions on economic topics; reached the semi-final of the 2025 regional tournament.
I like the stock market.Built and tracked a mock portfolio of 8 stocks over 3 months, writing a weekly note on each change.
I organised an event.Organised a charity football tournament for 80 students, raising 250,000 FCFA for a local orphanage.

Useful verbs

analysed, built, prepared, organised, led, presented, researched, managed, created, negotiated, raised, reduced, won

Avoid

"helped with", "was responsible for", "participated in", and full sentences starting with "I".

5. The cover letter

A cover letter answers three questions in under one page (about 300–400 words): why this firm, why this division, and why you. Many spring week applications replace it with 2–3 short motivation questions of 200–300 words each; the same structure works for both.

ParagraphPurposeWhat to write
1. IntroductionWho you are, what you're applying forProgramme name, year and city; your school or university; one strong result
2. Why this firmShow you did your research1–2 specific facts: a recent deal, a value in action, a conversation with someone who works there, an event you attended
3. Why this divisionShow you understand the jobWhat the division does in plain words, which part interests you, linked to something you did
4. Why youProve it with evidence2–3 examples from your CV, each with a skill and a result
5. CloseShort and politeThank them; say you would welcome the chance to discuss your application

Rules

  • Name the right firm and programme. A letter with another bank's name in it is rejected instantly.
  • The swap test: if a sentence would still be true with another firm's name in it, it is too generic. Replace it with a specific fact.
  • Open with "Dear Hiring Team" and end with "Yours faithfully". If you know the recruiter's name, write "Dear Ms Koné" and end with "Yours sincerely".
  • Find your facts on the firm's website, in recent news, and in your company profiles (Step 3).

"Why this firm": weak vs strong

Weak

"I want to work at your firm because it is a global leader with a strong culture and excellent training."

This passes the swap test for every bank in the world, so it tells the reader nothing.

Strong (fictional, school level)

"I first became interested in [Firm] at your online insight session for school students in March, where an analyst explained how the team helped a West African telecoms company raise capital. I had followed that company's results for my school investment club, and hearing how the deal was structured showed me how much of the work is research and clear communication, the two things I enjoy most in running our weekly market briefing."

What the CV does well

  • Numbers in almost every bullet: "12 UK business services companies", "300+ client portfolio statements", "from three hours to twenty minutes".
  • Results, not tasks: the comparables analysis "became the benchmarking section of a live sell-side pitch".
  • Clear progression: operations intern → spring insight → M&A summer internship. Each role builds on the last.
  • Extracurriculars with evidence: vice-captain "for a squad of twenty"; tutoring where "all six passed at grade 5 or above".
  • Honest skill levels: Python (basic), Bloomberg (basic).

What the cover letter does well

  • A specific "why this firm": it cites an insight afternoon and a story an associate told, not a line from the website.
  • Every claim is backed by a CV example, with what each one taught.
  • A plain, confident tone: no "I am passionate about finance", no exaggeration.
