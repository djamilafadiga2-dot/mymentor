Online Assessments

Most spring week applications are filtered by online tests before a human reads your CV. Here is what the tests look like, how to prepare, and where to practise.

1. Where the tests fit

The online assessment usually arrives by email straight after you submit your application, with a few days to complete it. Only candidates who score well move on to the video interview.

CV & cover letter→ Online assessment→ Video interview→ Assessment centre→ Offer

Because thousands of students apply, the score cut-off is high. Aim to be in the top part of the group, not just to pass. The good news: these tests reward practice more than talent.

2. The five types of test

TestWhat it looks likeHow to get better
Numerical reasoningTables and charts of business data; you calculate percentages, ratios, growth rates and currency conversions under time pressure.Practise percentage change and ratios until they are automatic. Always use a calculator and scrap paper.
Verbal reasoningA short passage, then a statement: True, False or Cannot say.Judge only on the passage, never your own knowledge. "Cannot say" is correct more often than you think.
Logical reasoningSequences of shapes or grids; find the rule and pick the next one.Learn the common rules (rotation, count, colour, position) and check one rule at a time.
Situational judgement (SJT)A workplace scenario; you rank or pick the best and worst responses.Read the bank's values first. Good answers are proactive, honest, team-minded and escalate problems early.
Game-basedShort games (balloons, memory, reaction) that measure traits like risk-taking and attention. Used by Pymetrics and Arctic Shores.There are no right answers. Try one practice game so the format is familiar, then play naturally.

Timing: in most ability tests you have roughly 30–50 seconds per question. Speed with accuracy is what separates candidates.

3. Which bank uses which test

Banks buy their tests from a handful of providers. Knowing the provider tells you what format to practise. Recent recruitment cycles have looked like this:

ProviderBanks that have used itFormat
Aon (cut-e)Morgan Stanley, UBS, BNP Paribas, NomuraVery short, fast numerical, verbal and logical tests
SHLDeutsche Bank, Barclays, JefferiesClassic numerical, verbal and inductive reasoning
CappfinityHSBCStrengths-based questions, often with a timer per item
PymetricsBlackstone, Standard CharteredGames
Korn Ferry (Talent Q)MacquarieAdaptive tests: questions get harder as you answer correctly

Providers change from year to year. Check your invitation email: it always names the platform, and that is the one to practise.

4. Your 4-week preparation plan

Start about a month before applications open (so August for a September launch), and aim for 30–45 minutes a day.

Week 1Diagnose

Take one free numerical, verbal and logical test each. Note your weakest.

Week 2Fix the basics

Percentages, ratios, reading charts; the True / False / Cannot say rules.

Week 3Timed practice

Full tests under real time limits, in the format of your target banks.

Week 4SJTs & games

Read each bank's values, practise SJTs, try one game-based test.

After each test, go through every wrong answer and write down why. Reviewing mistakes improves your score faster than taking more tests.

5. On the day

  • Do it at a desk, in a quiet room, on a laptop (not your phone), with a stable internet connection.
  • Have a calculator, pen and paper ready before you click start.
  • Complete it within 2–3 days of receiving it; some roles close as places fill.
  • Read the instructions page carefully: some tests penalise wrong answers, most don't. If they don't, never leave a question blank.
  • Don't get stuck: if a question takes too long, make your best guess and move on.
  • Do the test yourself. Tests are often repeated at the assessment centre, and help from anyone else is cheating.
