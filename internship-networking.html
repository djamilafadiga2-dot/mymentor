Internships · Step 5

Networking & Commercial Awareness

How to meet people in the industry, keep up with the markets, and turn a spring week into a summer internship offer.

Why bother

Talking to people who already do the job gives you the one thing applications need most: specific detail. A sentence like "an analyst in the industrials team told me…" cannot be copied from a website, and it is what makes a "why this firm" answer convincing. It also tells you whether you actually want the job.

You are not asking anyone for a job. You are asking for fifteen minutes of their time to hear about their work. Most people say yes to that, because someone did it for them.

Your LinkedIn profile first

Before you message anyone, make your profile worth clicking on:

  • A clear photo: plain background, smart clothes, looking at the camera.
  • Headline: what you are and what you're interested in. "Student at Lycée Sainte-Marie | Interested in finance and markets" beats "Student".
  • About: three or four sentences on what you study, what you've done, and what you want to explore.
  • Experience and activities: the same entries as your CV, so the two match.
  • Post occasionally: a short write-up of a project, an event you attended, or something you learned. One good post a month is plenty.

Finding the right people

Step 1Search

On LinkedIn, search the firm and filter by school, city or role (analyst, spring intern).

Step 2Look for a link

Same school, same country, same club, same degree. A shared link doubles your reply rate.

Step 3Start junior

Analysts and recent spring interns reply most and remember the process best.

Step 4Keep a record

Save who you spoke to and what they said in that firm's company profile in Step 3.

Also use what already exists: insight events and webinars run by the banks, alumni from your school, and your MyMentor mentor, who can often introduce you directly.

The message

Short, specific, easy to say yes to. Never attach your CV to a first message.

Subject: Question from a student in Abidjan interested in [division]

Hello [name],

I'm a student at [school] in Abidjan, hoping to study in the UK and work in finance. I saw that you [something specific and true: studied at the same school / work in the industrials team / joined through a spring week].

I co-founded our school's investment club and I've been following [firm or sector] since [specific reason]. I'd really like to understand what the work is actually like day to day.

Would you have 15 minutes in the next few weeks for a short call? I'm happy to fit around your schedule.

Thank you for your time,
[your name]

What not to send

  • "Hi, can you refer me for a spring week?" — asking for a favour before a conversation.
  • A long message about yourself with no question in it.
  • The identical message to twenty people. People at the same firm talk to each other.
  • Messaging again after two attempts. One follow-up after 7–10 days is polite; more is not.

On the call

  • Have four or five questions ready and keep to the time you asked for. End it yourself at 15 minutes; they will usually carry on if they want to.
  • Ask about their experience, not for things you could look up: how they chose the division, what surprised them, what a normal day looks like, what they'd do differently.
  • Take notes, and write them into the firm's profile in Step 3 the same day.
  • Send a thank-you message within 24 hours, naming one thing you took from the conversation.
  • Send a short update months later when you apply. That is what turns a contact into a supporter.

Commercial awareness

Commercial awareness means being able to talk about something happening in business right now, and say why it matters. Interviewers ask for it in one question: "Tell me about a deal or market story you've been following." You cannot revise for that the night before, but fifteen minutes a day for a few months is more than enough.

A routine that works

Daily · 10 minHeadlines

Read the business headlines. You only need to know what happened.

Weekly · 30 minOne story in depth

Pick one story and read two articles about it, ideally with different views.

Weekly · 10 minWrite it down

Log it below: what happened, and why it matters. Three sentences.

MonthlySay it out loud

Explain your best story to someone in 60 seconds without notes.

Depth beats breadth. Three stories you can discuss properly are worth far more than thirty headlines you half-remember. Follow one deal or one theme over several weeks, so you can talk about what happened next.

How to talk about a story

What happened. "Last month Company A agreed to buy Company B for about £3bn, mostly in shares."

Why they did it. "A wants B's distribution network in Asia, where A has almost no presence."

What you think. "It looks expensive against what similar companies have sold for, so it only works if the cost savings they promised are real. I'd want to see the first results after the deal closes."

Why it matters to the bank. "It also shows deal activity coming back in the sector, which is good for advisory teams like yours."

Having a view matters more than having the right view. Say what you think and why, and be willing to be argued with.

During the spring week

A spring week is not a holiday with free lunches. At most firms it is the first round of the summer internship process: the people running it decide who gets fast-tracked. Everything below is about being someone they remember for the right reasons.

Before it starts

  • Reread the firm's company profile in Step 3, and check the news from the last month.
  • Prepare your introduction: who you are, what you study, why you're here. You will say it twenty times.
  • Write down five questions you genuinely want answered during the week.
  • Check the dress code, the address and your travel. Arrive ten minutes early every day.

During the week

DoWhy
Ask questions in every sessionSpeakers report back on who engaged. One thoughtful question a session is the right amount: dominating is worse than silence.
Take notes and use namesWrite down who you met and what they said, the same day. Using someone's name on day three is remembered.
Be kind to everyoneRecruiters, assistants and current interns are all asked what they thought of you.
Help your groupIn group tasks, the person who organises the work and brings quiet people in scores better than the loudest.
Put the phone awayIt is the single most common complaint about spring interns.
Be careful at social eventsDrinks are still part of the assessment. Have a soft drink and enjoy the conversation.

Afterwards

  1. Within 24 hours: thank the people who hosted you, by email or LinkedIn, mentioning something specific from the week.
  2. Within a week: write up what you learned about each division, and which one you now want. This is the raw material for your summer internship application.
  3. Apply early for the summer internship. If you are offered a fast-track, take it and prepare properly: it is a real interview.
  4. Stay in touch: a short message twice a year is enough to be remembered.

If it doesn't lead to an offer, you still have a week inside the industry, several contacts and much better answers for the next application. Most people apply more than once. Ask for feedback, use it, and apply again.
